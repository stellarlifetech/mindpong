The research

In 2022, researchers at Cortical Labs grew hundreds of thousands of real neurons on a chip covered in electrodes and connected them to a simulated game of Pong, in an experiment called "DishBrain". The electrodes told the cells where the ball was, the cells' own activity moved the paddle, and the feedback they received depended on whether they hit or missed. The cultures played better within minutes, which challenged assumptions about what simple networks of brain cells can do [20, 14]. A Neuron commentary described this as a first step toward advanced drug screening and biological computing [21].

The field has grown quickly. In 2023, a "biocomputer" linking lab-grown human brain tissue to electronic hardware recognized voices [22]. Cortical Labs now offers a code-deployable biological computer (CL1) and a cloud platform for running code on living neurons [23], and high-density electrode arrays with thousands of electrodes let researchers stimulate and record neural networks in closed loops [24].

Why this model is used here

Pong gives a simple, measurable "behavior" for a network of neurons: a clear signal leads to good aim, and a disrupted network plays worse. Real neuron cultures need specialized labs, incubators and weeks of care, so this page uses a computer model that copies the DishBrain setup: 1,000 simulated neurons, a virtual 8×8 electrode grid, and the same sense, act and feedback loop. That lets you change one mechanism at a time, such as weaker brake cells or more background noise, and see the effect instantly and safely.

Why it matters for clinical psychiatry and nursing

Many psychiatric and neurological conditions are now understood as problems in how brain circuits balance excitation and inhibition and handle signals and noise. This simulation turns those hidden circuit changes into something you can see, hear and measure, then links them to what nurses do: recognizing cues, giving medications that act on circuits, using therapeutic communication, and evaluating outcomes. It mirrors how laboratory biocomputing platforms are being proposed for disease modeling and drug testing, translated into clinical judgment for nursing education. It remains a teaching model, not real neurons or a real patient.

How the game worksBall, paddles, circles, flashes, score and who winsOpen

Each paddle is moved only by its brain circuit, in a loop: sense (the ball's position reaches the circuit as electrical pulses) → respond (its neurons fire) → act (the paddle moves to where the response is strongest) → feedback (a hit or a miss sends a signal back to the circuit).

Ball
Its height is sent to 8 "eye" electrodes in each circuit; the pulses speed up as the ball gets closer.
Paddles
Left: the opponent. Right: your client. Each is drawn in its circuit's color.
Empty circles
Where each circuit "sees" the ball: its most active eye electrode. The paddle moves toward its circle. A circle that tracks the ball means a clear signal; one that wanders or lags means noise or bursts are blurring it.
Dashed center line
The net, as in table tennis. Each circuit defends its own half of the court.
Green and red flashes
A hit gives that circuit a short, regular pulse (predictable feedback). A miss gives it 1 second of random stimulation (unpredictable feedback), and the point goes to the other side. This feedback is how the DishBrain cultures learned.
Score
First to 7 points wins a match, celebrated with a fanfare and a golden flash on the winner's side; the tally below the score counts matches won. For a winner prediction, 3 matches are played: the winner is known only after the 3rd match, and the predicted player must win at least 2 of the 3. The bonus also needs the indicated care in all 3 matches: a fitting medication, the therapeutic response and all 4 health education topics for each player with a condition.

No ball is shown between points, during a miss penalty or after a match, so the circles disappear and both paddles drift back to the center. Both circuits are built from the same wiring, so the only differences between them are the condition and the care given: medications, therapeutic communication and health education. Who wins is decided by neural activity: each paddle's speed and reaction delay come from its circuit's live Neural control score (signal clarity, crowd firings, activity level and intact connections), and every crowd firing freezes the paddle briefly, like a lapse in awareness. Single matches still vary, as real performance does, so compare several. In our test runs the healthy client won most matches (usually 6 or more of every 8) against untreated epilepsy, schizophrenia, ADHD, depression or late Alzheimer's; partial treatment narrowed the gap, and full treatment (the right medication, a therapeutic response and all four health education topics) brought epilepsy and ADHD to about even, with schizophrenia close behind. A calm autism circuit also played about evenly.

How to read the monitorsReadouts, wave, spikes, EEG or action potentials?Spotting significant spikesOpen
Busy (Hz)
How many times each nerve cell fires per second, on average, over the last 10 seconds. It shows how active the whole circuit is. Healthy client: about 8. Much higher means overexcited; much lower means quiet or underactive.
Crowd firings /min
How often a huge crowd of cells (over a fifth of the circuit) fires in the same split second, counted over the last 10 seconds and shown per minute. These are seizure-like bursts. Healthy client: under 10. Hundreds point to seizure-like activity.
Signal clarity
How much more the cells that sense the ball fire than their neighboring sensing cells: how clearly the circuit "sees" where the ball is. Healthy client: about 2.3. Higher is clearer; close to 1 means the ball's signal is lost in background noise, so the paddle aims poorly.
Neural control
One number that sums up how well the circuit's measured activity can steer the paddle. It multiplies four live measures: signal clarity (can it tell where the ball is?), crowd firings (seizure-like bursts freeze the paddle for 0.2 seconds each), activity level (both too busy and too quiet hurt) and intact connections (lost synapses or cells). Healthy client: about 95 to 100%. Lower control means a slower paddle and a longer reaction delay (up to 0.4 seconds), so the match is decided by the neural activity, which the condition, the medications, your therapeutic communication and your health education all change.
Returned
The share of balls this circuit has hit back in the current match. It resets with every new match, including after a treatment change. Healthy client: about 80% over long runs. Early numbers swing a lot, so watch at least 8 to 10 balls.
Reading the wave against the faint line. The faint horizontal line behind each wave is the healthy reference level: about 8 firings per cell per second, the average activity of the healthy client. It sits at the same height on both monitors, so you can compare the two clients at a glance.
  • Wave mostly above the line: the simulation is busier than a healthy client's. You'll see this in the schizophrenia and ADHD simulations, and in epilepsy around its bursts.
  • Wave mostly below the line: quieter than healthy, as in the epilepsy simulation during its silent stretches just after a burst. The depression, autism and late Alzheimer's simulations, and circuits sedated by strong medication, sit below the line, which the game counts as underactive (slower reactions).
  • Wave hovering around the line: activity is in the healthy range.
Is this an EEG or action potentials? Neither exactly; it sits between the two.
  • Top sweep = action potentials. Each dot is one action potential (one "spike") from one of the 1,000 simulated nerve cells: a single all-or-none electrical event lasting about 1 millisecond.
  • Bottom wave = population firing rate. The wave counts all the action potentials in the circuit in each 10-millisecond window (firings per cell per second). That is closest to the multi-unit activity recorded by the electrode arrays under living neurons in DishBrain-style experiments.
  • Not a scalp EEG. An EEG records slow, summed currents from millions of cells through the skull, in microvolts, with rhythms such as alpha waves. This simulation has only 1,000 cells and no skull or scalp. Its patterns still map onto ideas you will see on an EEG: the yellow "Burst" flags behave like the sharp spike discharges of a seizure, and a flat, quiet trace resembles slowed activity.

How to spot what matters: ▼ Burst means over a fifth of all cells fired within 10 milliseconds: a seizure-like event that also freezes that player's paddle for 0.2 seconds. Orange line means the circuit is overactive (noise or weak brakes). A wave hugging the faint line is in the healthy range.

Top: the spike sweep, where each dot is one cell firing. Bottom: the neural activity wave, a colored line tracing how busy the whole circuit is, like a monitor waveform. The moving vertical line is the sweep: on the circuit you are listening to it is bright and marks the moment you are hearing; the picture is held back by a split second so it lines up exactly with the sound.

Sound layers:
What the sound layers meanNeuron noise, notes and burst knocks, game sounds, brake-cell zapsWhat you are hearingOpen
Neuron noise (background)
A soft, rain-like hiss that follows how many cells are firing, moment to moment. A calm, healthy simulation sounds like light rain; a busy one (ADHD, schizophrenia) sounds like heavier, steadier rain. Off by default; switch it on as an extra layer for study.
Notes and burst knocks
Notes: the 800 "go" cells are split into eight groups, each with its own musical note. A note plays when its group fires more than usual; several at once make a chord. Burst knocks: a short, soft knock each time a crowd firing happens (over a fifth of the circuit firing in the same split second), matching the yellow peaks in the wave.
Game sounds
Sounds from the game itself, not the neurons: a soft wooden "tock" when a paddle returns the ball, a gentle two-note slide down when a ball is missed, and a short, bright fanfare for the match winner. They play slightly to the left for the opponent and to the right for your client. On by default.
Brake-cell zaps
A short, soft arcade-style laser "pew" from the 200 "brake" (inhibitory) cells, as if they were zapping runaway activity. It sounds only when they suddenly work harder than usual to calm a rise in activity, so there is no constant hum. Frequent zaps mean the brakes are busy reining activity in; few zaps in a busy simulation suggest weakened brakes, as in epilepsy. Off by default.

    How busy (firings/cell/s)
    The average number of times each nerve cell fired per second. It tells you how active the whole circuit is. Healthy client: about 9. Much higher means an overexcited circuit; much lower means a quiet, underactive one.
    Big crowd firings /min
    How many times per minute a huge crowd of cells (over about a fifth of the circuit) fired within the same hundredth of a second. These are seizure-like bursts. Healthy client: under 10. Hundreds per minute point to seizure-like activity.
    Firing in lockstep (0–1)
    How closely the different areas of the circuit rise and fall together, measured as the average correlation between electrodes. 0 means each area acts independently; 1 means the whole circuit moves as one. Healthy client: about 0.02. High values mean the circuit has lost its independent "conversations", as in a seizure.
    Fast gamma rhythm %
    The share of the circuit's overall activity that pulses at 30 to 80 beats per second. In the brain, gamma rhythms are linked to attention and processing information, and changes in them are reported in conditions such as schizophrenia. Healthy client: about 16%. Higher or lower is a difference to notice, not good or bad on its own.
    Signal clarity
    How much more the cells that sense the ball fire than their neighboring sensing cells. It shows how clearly the ball's position stands out from background chatter. Healthy client: about 2.3 (the ball's cells fire 2.3 times as much). Higher is a clearer message; closer to 1 means the signal is lost in the noise.
    Pong hit rate %
    The share of balls the circuit returned. These recorded runs used the original paddle control, where about 25% is pure guessing, so the differences here are small and mostly luck. The live arena uses a newer paddle that follows signal clarity, which is why its hit rates are higher.

    Special focus lesson

    Safety first: when therapeutic communication is not the priority

    Open

    Key NCLEX principle: when a client presents an immediate threat to the nurse, the client or others, the nurse addresses safety first, before attempting therapeutic communication.

    1. Recognize the safety cue

    Ask yourself: "Is there an immediate danger that could cause serious injury or death?"

    • A client holding a firearm or other weapon
    • A client threatening to harm someone
    • Active violence or physical aggression
    • A client attempting to attack another person
    • Fire, hazardous chemicals or other immediate environmental dangers

    If the answer is yes, prioritize safety.

    2. Why talking may not be the best first action

    Therapeutic communication requires the nurse to engage with the client. When a client is armed or otherwise immediately dangerous, approaching or starting a conversation may:

    • Draw the client's attention to the nurse
    • Increase the nurse's exposure to danger
    • Escalate the situation
    • Give the client a chance to approach or target the nurse

    Do not confuse "calm communication" with "safe communication." A calm approach is not automatically the safest approach.

    3. NCLEX priority: safety before communication

    1. Recognize the threat.
    2. Do not approach or engage.
    3. Remove yourself from immediate danger.
    4. Obtain appropriate emergency assistance.
    5. Communicate therapeutically when it is safe to do so.
    Memory aid: S.A.F.E.
    S

    A

    Example: an armed client during a community visit

    A community mental health nurse pulls into an apartment parking lot for a scheduled visit and sees the client standing at the building entrance, waving a handgun and shouting at passersby. The nurse is still in the car. What should the nurse do?

    • Best choice Stay in the car, drive out of the parking lot to a safe location, then call emergency services and notify the agency. Leaving puts distance between the nurse and the armed client right away. Help is called once the nurse is out of danger, following agency policy.
    • Not the best choice Lower the window and calmly ask the client to put the gun down. A calm voice does not make it safe: speaking to the client draws their attention to the nurse and invites them to approach.
    • Not the best choice Park in the lot and call emergency services from the car. Calling for help is correct, but staying parked within sight and range of the client keeps the nurse in danger.
    • Not appropriate Flash the headlights so the client knows the nurse has arrived. A sudden signal draws attention, may startle the client and can escalate the situation.

    This example was written for this page to illustrate the principle; it is not taken from any published test bank.

    De-escalate when it is safe to engage

    An agitated client who is pacing, shouting or clenching fists, with no weapon and no attack under way: approach calmly, keep more than an arm's length away, make sure both of you can reach an exit, use a low voice and offer choices. This is where therapeutic communication and de-escalation are the right first step.

    Remove yourself when it is not

    A weapon, an attack in progress, a threat about to be carried out, or an environmental hazard such as fire: do not approach or engage. Get to safety, get emergency help according to agency policy, and return to therapeutic communication only once it is safe.

    Remember this NCLEX rule: immediate danger means safety first. Do not automatically choose "use therapeutic communication." Ask instead, "Can I safely communicate with this client right now?" If the answer is no, the priority is to remove yourself from danger and obtain appropriate help. Therapeutic communication is a nursing intervention, not a substitute for immediate physical safety.

    Adapted from [25]: Hernandez, J. P. (2026, October 7). Safety first: When therapeutic communication is not the priority [NCLEX teaching guide].

    Communication blocks (eliminate these)

    • False reassurance: "Don't worry, everything will be fine." Dismisses the feeling and promises what can't be guaranteed.
    • "Why" questions: "Why did you do that?" Demands justification and makes the client defensive.
    • Giving advice: "You should…", "If I were you…". Takes over the client's decision.
    • Approving or disapproving: "That's good" or "You shouldn't feel that way." Judges the client.
    • Agreeing or disagreeing: "You're right" or "That isn't true." Shuts down exploration.
    • Belittling feelings: "Everyone feels like that sometimes."
    • Changing the subject: "Let's talk about something nicer."
    • Passing the buck: "Ask your doctor about that." Closes the conversation and avoids the feeling.
    • Arguing with delusions or hallucinations: "There are no voices." Damages trust.
    • Exploring hallucination details: "Are the voices male or female?" Reinforces them.
    • Stereotyped comments and clichés: "Keep your chin up."
    • Lying or harsh reorientation in dementia: "Your mother died years ago." Causes fresh grief.
    • Focusing on the nurse: sharing the nurse's own experiences or feelings.

    Test-taking strategy: choosing the best therapeutic response

    1. Find what the question is asking.Look for "most therapeutic", "best response", "priority" or "needs further teaching". A "further teaching" or "non-therapeutic" stem flips the task: you are looking for the wrong statement.
    2. Check safety first.If there is immediate danger (a weapon, an attack, a threat about to be carried out, fire), the answer is to stay out of danger and get help, not to talk; see Safety first and the S.A.F.E. aid. If the client hints at suicide, self-harm, harm to others, command hallucinations or abuse, the correct answer addresses safety directly, for example by asking about suicide. Safety beats every other technique.
    3. Cross out the communication blocks.Eliminate false reassurance, "why" questions, advice, judging, arguing, changing the subject and passing the buck. Usually two or three options fall away at once.
    4. Keep the focus on the client's feelings.Prefer an option that acknowledges or reflects the client's emotion over one that gives information, teaches or talks about the nurse.
    5. Choose the response that keeps the conversation open.Open-ended questions, reflection and clarifying invite the client to say more; closed or yes-no questions usually don't, unless you are assessing safety.
    6. Match the response to the condition.Psychosis: present reality without arguing and assess for command hallucinations. Dementia: validate and redirect, don't correct. Autism: calm, concrete words and less stimulation. Anxiety or agitation: short, simple statements in a calm, low-stimulation space. Depression: ask directly about suicide.
    7. Two options still look right?Pick the one that is client-centered and invites further talk. For select-all-that-apply items, judge each option on its own as true or false.

    Worked example

    A client with depression says, "Everyone would be better off without me." Options: (A) "You have so much to live for." (B) "Why would you say that?" (C) "Don't talk like that." (D) "Are you thinking about killing yourself?"

    Step 2, safety: the statement suggests suicidal thinking, so safety comes first. Step 3, blocks: A is false reassurance, B is a "why" question, and C is disapproving, so all three go. Answer: D, asking directly about suicide.

    Practice this in the simulation: choose each response in the "Talk with your client" card above and watch how the client's distress changes the simulated neural activity and the match.

The questions and answer choices are shuffled for every attempt. You have 1.5 minutes per question: the test moves on automatically when time runs out, and you cannot go back to a previous question, as on the real exam. You can pause and save and finish within 24 hours of the first pause. A correct winner prediction in the game (your pick wins at least 2 of 3 matches, with the indicated medication, therapeutic response and health education in place for each player with a condition) earns a one-time bonus, added to your first attempt. All your completed attempts are averaged, and that average sets your level of achievement. Before the test unlocks, play a full 3-match prediction series, run the simulation for at least 3 matches with both players (the opponent and your client) given a medication, a therapeutic communication response and at least one health education topic (any choice counts; trial and error is welcome), and check off the NCLEX test plan map and the therapeutic communication lessons (Safety first, techniques, blocks and the test-taking strategy). Read the rules on the start screen before you begin. After you answer, every option is marked correct or incorrect with its rationale.

    A computer model of 1,000 connected nerve cells, based on laboratory research in which real neurons grown on electrode arrays (the "DishBrain" experiment) learned to play Pong. Each client on this page is one circuit.
    "Go" and "brake" cells
    Excitatory cells push neighbors to fire; inhibitory cells calm them. Healthy activity needs both in balance.
    Crowd firing (burst)
    A huge group of cells firing at once. Many per minute look like seizure activity.
    Signal clarity
    How much more the cells that "see" the ball fire than their neighbors. Higher means a clearer message.
    Proxy
    A stand-in. When the circuit lacks a brain chemical (like dopamine), we show a drug's effect as a similar change the circuit can show, and say so.

    Abbreviations

    Open
    ADHD
    Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: a neurodevelopmental condition involving persistent inattention, hyperactivity and/or impulsivity.
    NMDA
    N-methyl-D-aspartate: a type of glutamate receptor that helps nerve cells learn and that switches on brake (inhibitory) cells. Underactive NMDA receptors are a leading hypothesis in schizophrenia.
    STDP
    Spike-timing-dependent plasticity: the learning rule in the model. A connection strengthens when one cell fires just before the next, and weakens when the order is reversed. The "Learning speed" slider changes it.
    ANC
    Absolute neutrophil count: a blood test monitored with clozapine.
    ARIA
    Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities: brain swelling or small bleeds seen on MRI with anti-amyloid antibodies.
    CDR-SB
    Clinical Dementia Rating, Sum of Boxes: a scale used to measure decline in Alzheimer's trials.
    GABA
    Gamma-aminobutyric acid: the brain's main calming (inhibitory) chemical messenger. Benzodiazepines strengthen its effect.
    IV / PRN
    Intravenous (into a vein) / pro re nata (as needed).
    LPN/VN, RN, PN
    Licensed practical/vocational nurse, registered nurse, practical nurse.
    MRI
    Magnetic resonance imaging.
    NCLEX, NCSBN, NGN
    National Council Licensure Examination; National Council of State Boards of Nursing; Next Generation NCLEX.
    NMS
    Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: a rare, life-threatening reaction to antipsychotics (fever, rigidity, unstable vital signs).
    REMS
    Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy: an FDA drug-safety program.
    SSRI
    Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, such as sertraline or escitalopram.
    SV2A
    Synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A: the target of levetiracetam, and a marker used to measure synapse density on PET (positron emission tomography) scans.
    FDA
    U.S. Food and Drug Administration.
    NAMI
    National Alliance on Mental Illness: a U.S. organization offering mental health education, support groups and the Family-to-Family program.
    CHADD
    Children and Adults with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder: a support and education organization.
    DBSA
    Depression and Bipolar Support Alliance: peer support groups for mood disorders.
    CBT
    Cognitive behavioral therapy: a structured talk therapy for depression and other conditions.
    ACT
    Assertive community treatment: a team that provides intensive mental health care in the community.
    IEP
    Individualized education program: a plan for special education services at school.

    What this can and cannot tell us

    Open

    This is a teaching model of 1,000 simplified cells, not a brain or a person. Each condition tests one scientific idea about circuits. Several medications act on chemicals the circuit doesn't have, so they appear as labeled proxies. The link between a nurse's words and the circuit's noise is a teaching metaphor: in real care, therapeutic communication works through trust, safety and coping. Nothing here can diagnose, treat or replace clinical guidance, drug references or your program's policies.
